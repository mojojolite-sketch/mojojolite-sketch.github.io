### 一、先补齐二手公众号完全漏掉的“暗线”：两位作者是谁？这篇文章真正的经济学底牌是什么？

微信公号（金刚若木8）在文末评析第一条嘲讽称：“两位作者都是 OpenAI 员工，却拿‘二十四岁带团队’的内部样本推及所有行业”，并在第五条批评全文“对劳动力测量缺乏实证支撑”。这是典型的只看字面、不查作者学术履历造成的二手误读：

1. 作者真实背景（Harvard 制度经济学与企业理论学派传人）：
   - 本文作者 Hemanth Asirvatham 与 Elliott Mokski 并非普通软件工程师，而是 OpenAI 经济研究团队（Economic Research）研究员，两人与哈佛大学经济学巨擘 Andrei Shleifer（全球被引用次数最高的经济学家之一，现代公司治理、不完全契约与企业边界理论的奠基人之一）于 2026 年 2 月联合发表了著名的 NBER Working Paper No. 34834：《GPT as a Measurement Tool》，并开发了 OpenAI 开源定性转定量测量库 GABRIEL（*Generalized Attribute Based Ratings Information Extraction Library*）。
   - 在 NBER WP 34834 中，三人完成了两项直接支撑本文的核心实证：
     - 实证 1（定性工作与文本的可验证测量）：在 1,000+ 个人工标注的定性评估任务上验证 GPT 模型可将非结构化人类语言、演讲、文档转化为高信度定量评分（与人类专家打分在统计上不可区分，且对 Prompt 措辞扰动高度稳健）；
     - 实证 2（37,000 项历史技术的采用时滞压缩）：构建涵盖 37,000 项历史技术的数据库，证明从“技术发明”到“企业规模化采用”的时滞已从工业革命早期的 约 50 年 压缩 10 倍至现代的 约 5 年。
2. 系列上下文（《The Next Economy》双篇闭环）：
   - 本文是 OpenAI *Intelligence Age* 专栏「下一个经济（The Next Economy）」系列的第二篇（2026-10-28）。在 第一篇《The eternal complement》（2026-10-01） 中，两位作者提出了 *“制度智能（Institutional Intelligence）”* 的核心概念：
     > “A great idea must survive a long chain of correct local actions across these institutions. Call this institutional intelligence: the uncelebrated intelligence of execution. Genius designs the monument; institutions lay the stone... We sometimes imagine superintelligence as a billion Einsteins. But a civilization of a billion Einsteins still needs most of them to mine the quarries and manage the accounting.”
   - 读懂了 Shleifer 的不完全契约传统 + NBER WP 34834（把不可验证的定性工作变成可验证指标）+ 第一篇的“制度执行互补品”，才会明白《The Future of the Firm》根本不是散文，而是一篇用现代语言重写科斯（Coase 1937）、霍姆斯特朗（Holmström 1979 道德风险与可观测性）、格罗斯曼-哈特-摩尔（GHM 1986/1990 不完全契约与纵向一体化）与加里卡诺（Garicano 2000 知识层级）的制度经济学宣言。

### 三、真正犀利的一手批判（`/r` 深度审视）：走出二手公号的表层吐槽，原文真正的 5 大理论盲区与利益底牌在哪？

如果站在制度经济学、组织社会学与企业软件实战的高度审视 Hemanth Asirvatham 与 Elliott Mokski 的英文原稿，这篇文章真正值得深究的软肋绝不是二手公号说的那几条，而是以下 5 个深层结构性悖论：

#### 1. 「劳动自我入职（Labor onboards itself）」的哈耶克隐性知识盲区：把“Slack 留痕”当成“企业真相”
- 原文核心假设：作者提出 “Firms must learn technologies, but labor learns the firm and onboards itself”，并在第三部分（*The Layering*）设想 CEO Asha 的 AI 咨询顾问通过通读几百个 Slack 频道、文档与代码库，就能看穿整个公司的运作痛点。
- 深层批判（Polanyi 隐性知识 / Hayek 地方性知识）：
  在真实企业中，越是决定生死的核心约束，越不会写在 Slack 公开频道或 Confluence 文档里——例如：某大客户高管的政治忌讳、监管合规的灰色默契、部门间心照不宣的历史包袱、甚至线下茶水间与加密通话达成的妥协。AI 顾问读到的几百个 Slack 频道只是企业的*“数字化表象（Codified Trace）”*，而非企业的*“完整暗默上下文（Tacit Context）”*。当高管过度依赖一个只读表面留痕的“内部 AI 记者”直接跨级调配工程师时，极易因缺失水面下的隐性上下文而做出灾难性的机械干预。

#### 2. 古德哈特定律 2.0（Goodhart's Law in Semantic Surveillance）：当“AI 内部记者”常态化，Slack 将变成“面向大模型的提示词表演剧场”
- 原文核心假设：作者痛批上一代算法管理（测鼠标移动、算敲击次数、看绿点在线）导致了“表演性忙碌（performative productivity）”，认为引入“能读懂工作实质的 AI 监工（a monitor that understands）”并像车载保险追踪器一样实时汇报给 CEO（如发现居家办公的 Sarah 默默搭建了有用基建并予以提拔），就能终结表演性工作。
- 深层批判（Holmström & Milgrom 1991 多任务委托代理博弈）：
  作者低估了人类组织对任何监控指标的策略性反制（Adversarial Gaming）。一旦员工知道有一个 24 小时在线的 AI Consultancy 正在实时扫描所有 Slack 线程、Git 提交和文档，并直接向 CEO 生成晋升与追责建议：
  1. 真实沟通地下化（Off-Channel Migration）：销售与工程团队绝不会再在 Slack 上诚实暴露“某功能可能延期或存在架构缺陷”（因为会被 AI 立即抓包捅给 CEO Asha），所有实质性风险讨论将迅速逃逸至不受监控的私密语音或线下渠道，导致企业数字记忆彻底空心化；
  2. 语义级表演性忙碌（Semantic Performative Productivity / LLM SEO）：员工不再摇晃鼠标，而是学会用大模型喜欢的格式在 Slack 和代码库里批量制造“看似主动超额贡献”的文档与冗余基建（迎合 AI 监工的打分偏好）。从“糊弄笨算法”升级为“用 AI 糊弄 AI 监工”，组织内耗反而可能指数级膨胀。

#### 3. 「学徒制消亡（Death of Apprenticeship）」的跨期动态不一致：第一代 Jace 靠手搓表格攒出了品味，第二代 Jace 从哪习得纠错直觉？
- 原文内部的跨期逻辑冲突：
  - 在第一节讲会计 Jace 时，作者承认：“高管根本不知道怎么把财务数据拼起来，得有 Jace 这层领域专业知识（domain expertise），才能把智能体用好”，并在 *Why adoption might start at the bottom* 中强调：“最能指挥 AI 把活干好的人，恰恰是过去亲手干过这活的人（the person who used to do it themselves）”。
  - 然而紧接着在 *Entry-level is no longer the bottom* 中，作者却轻描淡写地宣称：人类最底层的苦活（grunt work）将彻底交给 AI，虽然这会损失学徒制（apprenticeship）和在杂活中试错成长的路径（learning-by-grunt-work），但新人可以直接在更高层级锻炼“判断力、品味与管理肌肉（judgment, taste, the muscles of management）”。
- 深层批判（认知科学的技能习得悖论）：
  在会计、法律、芯片设计、工业制造与临床医学中，高阶的“品味与异常判断力（Taste & Exception Handling）”从来不是脱离底层执行的空中楼阁，而是亲手踩过上千个底层脏数据、修过无数个边缘 Bug 后沉淀下来的神经直觉！在转型的第 1 年（$t_0$），24 岁的作者和老会计 Jace 能管好 AI，是因为他们自己或身边的前辈亲手写过代码、搓过表格；到了第 10 年（$t_1$），如果一整代新人从入职第一天起就只当“AI 微型公司的董事会主席”而不碰底层细节，当 AI 团队在复杂边界条件下产生极具迷惑性的系统性幻觉时，顶层人类将彻底丧失独立审计与证伪（Verification）的能力——“人类董事会”将名存实亡，沦为 AI 产出的橡皮图章。

#### 4. 法定责任与破产隔离边界（Fiduciary Duty & Liability Partitioning）：为什么“AI 麦肯锡”替代不了真正的麦肯锡与中层高管？
- 原文核心假设：作者认为传统管理咨询公司只是高管花高价雇来的“雇佣兵中间人（mercenary middlemen）”，而 ChatGPT 演进出的 AI Consultancy 既便宜又全天候在线，将顺理成章接管这一夹层。
- 深层批判（公司法与契约追责视角）：
  在真实商业世界中，CEO 雇佣麦肯锡或设立人类 VP 层级，至少有一半付费买的是“政治背书与责任分担（Blame-Shifting & Fiduciary Accountability）”，而不仅仅是“信息汇总”。当董事会或股东质询一项重大转型失败时，“这是麦肯锡尽调建议的”或“解雇分管 VP”是现代公司治理释放压力的法定安全阀；而一个没有独立法人资格、没有资产负债表承担连带赔偿责任的 AI Consultancy，无法替高管承担任何法律与受托责任（Fiduciary Duty）。只要最终签字坐牢或丢掉职位的仍是人类，人类高管就绝不可能像原文设想的那样轻易拆掉提供责任缓冲的人类管理层级。

#### 5. OpenAI 的终极商业底牌与“科斯敲竹杠（Hold-Up）”反噬：如果“劳动学会了公司”，这笔组织记忆资产归谁所有？
- 看穿 OpenAI 的商业叙事：
  为什么 OpenAI 要专门推出 *Intelligence Age* 平台并发表这篇《企业的未来》？因为文章的核心金句——*“Firms learn technology; labor learns the firm. The best labor onboards itself”*——正是 OpenAI 从 *“卖 \$20~\$200/月的软件工具席位（抢占企业 IT 预算，仅占 GDP 3%~5%）”* 迈向 *“直接按端到端部门职能与管理咨询收费（抢占企业人力薪酬与麦肯锡咨询预算，占企业支出 50%~70%）”* 的理论动员令！
  - 如果 AI 只是窄工具，企业买 Copilot 席位就够了，且落地慢；
  - 如果高管相信“范围越大越好采用（bigger scope is easier to adopt）”，高管就会直接从顶层采购 OpenAI 的*“端到端部门级 Agent 团队（Interleaving）”*与*“全公司级 AI 咨询中枢（Layering）”*。
- 科斯不完全契约的终极回旋镖（Vendor Hold-Up Problem）：
  恰恰按照作者自己在第四部分引用的航空航天公司与喷气发动机供应商的“不完全契约与敲竹杠（Hold-Up）”理论：如果一家企业真的让外部闭源大模型厂商的 Agent 团队“自我入职（onboards itself）”，吞吐并内化了全公司几百个 Slack 频道、代码库、客户底牌与核心工作流记忆，那么企业最核心的组织资本（Organizational Capital）就从人类员工脑中转移到了模型厂商的闭源上下文与权重生态里！一旦模型厂商提价、修改策略或发生数据泄露，企业将面临比“外包喷气发动机”严重百倍的供应商锁定（Vendor Lock-in）。这正是为什么当前精明的企业架构师都在自建模型无关的 Context/Harness 编排层与本地知识图谱，绝不敢把“公司的大脑皮层与中枢神经”直接交由单一模型商托管。

The future of the firm
How AI might enter the org chart—beneath, beside, and between us.
By Hemanth Asirvatham and Elliott Mokski
October 28, 2026

Authors' note: This is the second essay in our series on the next economy, part of a new platform to host independent voices exploring an AGI future. It reflects our views, not those of OpenAI or our colleagues.

The two of us were twenty-four when we started managing our own teams here at OpenAI. That is less impressive once you meet our teams.

We're supposed to be grunts. At twenty-four, you're at the bottom of the corporate ladder. You take the meeting notes and write a few memos. After years of drudgerous work, you are (hopefully) granted a broader remit.

Our path—and the path of many young people today—is looking different. We enter the workforce already with “employees” at our behest. These deputies work day and night, weekends and holidays. They are great at the grunt work. We even have math olympiad gold medalists on staff to advise us. Our new reports still make their share of mistakes. But they are getting better, learning our needs, and we are learning how to manage them.

Neither of us climbed the corporate ladder. Management has instead come down to our rung. This essay is about our promotion, and the other ways firms might reorganize as we enter the intelligence age.

When AI enters the firm, where does it go? We present three illustrative scenarios:
[Figure caption: Three organization charts show AI beneath human workers, beside human workers, and between layers.]

- Beneath us. Everyone keeps their seat, and a new basement of AI labor grows underneath. We all become managers. First we get assistants, then chiefs of staff, and eventually entire miniature firms reporting up to each of us. Every person gets a promotion.
- Beside us. AI joins the org chart alongside people as a colleague of sorts, working directly for existing human managers and executives. It starts out by taking up this task and that role. In time, it performs broader functions, reporting higher up the flagpole.
- Between us. The most unintuitive case: humans above and humans below, with AI in between. It coordinates and oversees work, both carrying out executives' intent and recognizing unique contributions made by enterprising employees.

None of these scenarios will play out exactly as we describe, but we think aspects of all three will come true. All three are already happening in subtle ways.

Our focus here isn't total automation but a transitioning economy. We are interested in what chimeric firms will look like as AI improves and laces itself into existing organizations.

We believe the hybrid firm is conceptually important because productive adoption of AI isn't instantaneous. When models only do a subset of tasks, they may be most smoothly adopted by the ground-level employees already familiar with those tasks. Once models can do broad functions end-to-end, executives can adopt them directly. In our view, more capable AI is more adoptable: firms must learn technologies, but labor learns the firm and onboards itself.

Below we unpack these three new org charts, and what they mean for red tape, worker agency, firm size, and pay disparity.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
I. The Promoting
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Our first scenario seems least disruptive. Here, people stay in the same seats with the same reporting lines, ostensibly doing the same jobs as before. The human org chart looks unchanged between today and this future.

The difference is below us. Beneath each individual contributor, a new AI labor force reports up to each of us. Our org chart gets a basement, and we all become managers.

At first, this basement is unfinished. We start with one or two agents apiece, less as seasoned employees, more as eager interns we must supervise and readily forgive. Our expectations are low: send an email here, draft a memo there, reply to Slack messages everywhere. Like junior human employees, they begin with little autonomy and trust.

But as models improve, our agents take on more tasks with less supervision. They coordinate with each other. There might be manager AIs and grunt AIs, accountants and lawyers, all working together at your behest. Far from handholding an intern, your operation eventually looks like a small firm in its own right, with its own org chart and you as its board of directors.

Jace still does the accounting at his firm, but he's no longer clicking around the spreadsheets. His agent underlings do that. Jace's job is more like his manager's from a few years ago. His manager, in turn, now has an even bigger remit.

Jace's day-to-day has completely changed, now focused on communication, coordination, and creativity. He works less and feels more challenged. His work life has more variance, and he distinguishes himself with leadership skills and a vision he couldn't showcase before.

The accounting team hasn't shrunk because the rest of the company has grown more productive too and demands more accounting than ever before. The agents can't yet do everything his team does. The execs wouldn't know the first thing about assembling financial data and need his domain expertise to use the agents effectively.

### Automation without pink slips

Notice how this scenario doesn't imply layoffs. Here we adopt AI without automating ourselves. We instead automate our past selves, and we move up in the hierarchy. AI takes on more and more of what we used to do. Our jobs remain because there is more to manage, and we gain responsibility for a greater sum of output.

Jobs aren't static. Suppose that AI can do ninety percent of what you did before. You might conclude you're left with only ten percent of the work. The other possibility is that you're still doing a full job, just a different one—now focused on an objective ten times larger. That is the classic gift of technology: productivity rises, ambition rises to meet it, and wages can rise with the ambition.

This all depends on whether we still add value to production. This essay focuses on the coming years where automation isn't universally capable or adopted. Even if AI could do it all, we might still value something more because a person made it, or insist that a person have the final say.

It also depends on our appetite. If, as consumers, we don't care for ten times more output, the equilibrium will shift toward fewer workers or fewer hours worked. Historically, increases in output have not satiated us. We have a lot more but work only a little less. Should our appetite continue to grow as output multiplies—and we remain additive to production—we will choose to keep trading our labor for more consumption. If instead production becomes so abundant that we are satiated, we might volunteer a smaller labor force.

There are more objections we discuss later (e.g. whether we'll have a larger number of smaller firms). Still, we think this scenario matters because adoption has to start somewhere. Firms need a stable evolutionary path from their current structure. Supercharging existing workers' jobs may be an easier first step than a wholesale reorganization. We see this today, as individual after individual delegates to AI while the organizational revolution has yet to arrive.

### Why adoption might start at the bottom

This scenario relies on the front line hiring first. Ground-level workers—not managers and executives—lead on AI adoption. That appears likely, so long as the person who best gets AI to do the work is the person who used to do it themselves.

Models can already automate many data science tasks, but a department head unfamiliar with the methods and minutiae will struggle to leverage the tools. Until AI becomes proactive enough to tell you what you don't know (see our next scenario), it will probably work best for those already familiar with how the work is done.

### Entry-level is no longer the bottom

The biggest change in this scenario is for entry-level workers. There would no longer be a lowest rung, at least not for humans. When we hire young people, it won't be to place them at the drudgerous bottom of the firm. Those positions will be filled by something that doesn't mind.

With that, we lose some traditional routes to educate young workers: the apprenticeship, the learning-by-grunt-work, the earning of stripes. These are genuine costs, but we mostly see this as a privilege. The greenest hire with a good idea has resources to experiment. The oldest excuse in the building, we just don't have the manpower, is dying. A junior employee with a great video game in her head and no path to the headcount can now simply build it.
[Figure caption: Unfinished clay bowls and finished ceramic bowls sit on a potter's workbench.]

In our view, this “promoting” scenario still has much for junior workers to learn—judgment, taste, the muscles of management and creative vision. We will be incentivized to cultivate these skills because they show up in our work product. We will still learn by doing, but at a higher level of scope. Firm elders remain incentivized to help us as responsible stewards (with equity stakes in our success). The opportunity here is a more stimulating role for everyone at every rung. Anyone can showcase their talent across a broader remit, which is especially important for the newcomers who previously had no remit at all.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
II. The Interleaving
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

AI's second way into the firm weaves through the existing org chart. Here the agents aren't dregs but colleagues. A traditional manager hands a slice of the team's work to a model the way she might delegate to any employee. AI inserts itself next to humans, not just under them.

The key difference from the first scenario: here, productive AI is adopted by existing human managers, not only by current individual contributors. To do so, it must be competent enough to be a fully fledged team member.

Sheila is a VP at a furniture company. She has never run an ad campaign. She gives an AI marketing team a budget and a new sofa to sell. It divides the launch tasks among its own agents: some interview the salespeople, others coordinate with the factory on what they can actually deliver. One subagent launches an online survey while a few more prototype different visual styles.

The drafts and revisions pass between them without crossing her desk. Her human sales and design teams still report to her, alongside the team of marketing agents. On Monday, she is handed a campaign to approve and a question about whether the photos make the sofa look too blue. She would never have realized that sort of detail without an ad expert proactively flagging it. She authorizes the agents to coordinate with the human photographer on a reshoot.

### The escalator of scope

Humans are generalists. A hammer helps with one task; a human does a whole lot more. AI's journey from tool to labor is a widening ambit: automating the task, then the job, then a team, and finally a firm. Every step up in scope requires broader competence.

A job is a suitcase full of many tasks. For a whole team, that suitcase is crammed with all sorts of diverse work. More tasks in the remit mean more potential chokepoints. If even one essential task in the team involves physical labor, a whole new set of peripherals is required. To be a slot-in substitute for an entire team, the AI must be capable across all its varied responsibilities.

AI that can handle bigger scope is harder to build. But for firms—and this is the central asymmetry of the whole transition—bigger scope is easier to adopt.

An end-to-end automated team or broad function can be directly adopted by an executive. Narrow tools cannot be dropped in from above like that. Simple tools require company-wide buy-in and company-wide retraining.

### Firms learn technology; labor learns the firm

New technologies traditionally make it cheaper to do a confined set of tasks. Then the burden shifts to the buyer. The firm must figure out how to leverage the new tool, and must retrain, reorganize, and rebuild its processes around it. Spreadsheet software didn't install itself into the accounting department—the accounting department reshaped itself around the software.

Labor is the opposite. An intern, an employee, and a good team figure out how to be useful to you. They interrogate you, absorb your context, notice your needs. They tell you what you didn't even realize you didn't know. The best labor onboards itself.

The more AI becomes like labor in this sense, with initiative and adaptability, the less the firm must learn how to harness it and the more it figures out how to be useful to the firm. An executive probably won't sit through a design software 101 lecture to learn a new tool. But he will happily entertain an offer that sounds like: say the word, and I'll set up your whole marketing function.

### This isn't a lone-wolf economy

Too often, we name the holy grail of AGI as automating a job. Granted, we haven't even reached that yet. Jobs are so extraordinarily complicated because we are so impressively capable. Even so, very little of our economy's output comes from lone-wolf workers. Productive jobs contribute to teams, teams to firms, and firms produce most everything we consume.

The “job” standard for AGI doesn't give us humans enough credit. Labor isn't just about the tasks, or even handling the non-routine components: our labor is also about prodigious coordination, our plasticity to do small parts of bigger outputs. Selling us as mere sacks of tasks is pennies on the dollar. Human capacity is in our organizations more than the individual. An economic AGI must reach the standard of people, not person.

As a result, the “interleaving” scenario may be quite inefficient if the AI is still tool-like or even worker-like, instead of team-like or firm-like. A model wedged beside every worker means a thousand seams and handoffs. This piecemeal setup may slow adoption.

We see a race between earlier, less capable implementations and later, more capable models. The victor hinges on whether adoption of future models is so much faster that it overcomes the extra time it takes to reach those competencies.

### The two scenarios so far

This “interleaving” scenario can coexist with the first “promoting” scenario. We may see firms shrink their human footprint as AI automates certain functions, while the remaining humans get much broader mandates aided by their agent workforces. Importantly, by itself this scenario doesn't imply lower overall human employment. We could find ourselves with more firms, each with a lower headcount.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
III. The Layering
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Our third scenario is the most confounding. Here, AI enters the firm above people. The stack is no longer humans over machines but human, AI, human: an automation sandwich. Some people “report” to an AI.

It sounds far-fetched, especially as a starting point for firm adoption. But we think, in less radical ways, this has already begun.

Asha, the chief executive, is pinged by her AI consultancy that there is a problem with an upcoming launch. It has already investigated the issue and found that sales promised a feature engineering isn't building. The evidence is pulled from ordinary work threads in Slack; nobody has written her a report or even realized the miscommunication between the teams.

The AI consultancy proposes she delay the launch and reassign three engineers. It also notices that Sarah, a work-from-home engineer, has already built some useful infrastructure for the project even though no one told her to. The consultancy ensures Sarah's proactive contribution is recognized and communicated to Asha. Asha calls Sarah, thanks her for going above and beyond, and commends her by designating her the engineering lead of the new effort.

### The ladder might burn from the top

Even when we think of AI as a mechanizer of cognitive labor, we tend to focus on the lower rungs. We typically say AI automates spreadsheets and slide decks, not management. Our instinct is that automation climbs the hierarchy: cognitive gruntwork first and judgment last, with the manager as the final redoubt.

But a manager's work largely lives on a computer and in the mind: reading, meeting, weighing, holding context, making decisions. There is value to physical presence, to touring the office floors and being around the troops. Yet it is ground-level work, even when it is mostly cognitive work, that tangles with atoms and tools more than management. The intern is sent to the photocopier, not his boss.

Managerial decision-making is hard to teach to a model, because success is ambiguous and measured over long time horizons. It is for similar reasons that training good human managers is hard. We don't yet know how good the models are at management. Our evaluations abound for execution-focused cognitive labor, but few capture large-scale orchestration at the scope of managers and execs.

Still, it is worth considering that today's models may be closer in capability to human managers than to ground-level workers. Some managerial functions might be automated before the work they oversee.

### Consultants everywhere, all the time

Perhaps the full version of this “layering” scenario is an AI that does the thinking, then fills its capability gaps with human contractors who report to it. That world seems quite foreign to us. A lot would need to change.

But there is a more organic incarnation, and its opening act is already onstage: advising. Today, an exec can ask ChatGPT to read hundreds of Slack channels, documents, missives, and code files from across her company, and then answer questions. What does this team really do? Where is that vulnerability showing up? Every part of the org is increasingly parsable to her.

Executives have long laced the org chart with mercenary middlemen—they called it consulting. A management consultancy layers you, briefly and expensively. Going forward, AI consultants may be negligibly cheap and ever-present.

Models are just beginning to reliably parse firm documents and communications. Their taste and proactivity must mature. For now, ChatGPT helps a motivated exec understand her org in greater detail. We see it eventually becoming a full-service consultancy. It won't be a true “manager” of lower-level employees, but rather an in-house correspondent and advising partner to executives. It will leverage its business acumen to review everything, proactively pointing out trouble spots and helping prioritize.

Whether this is for better or worse depends on the executive. The structural effect is centralizing. We believe this will shorten the distance between top management and the actual operations of the firm. It concentrates more power at the top, whereas previously, frictions in information flow favored more decentralized decision-making.

We find this scenario profound not only because it shifts the balance of power, but because “AI as consultancy” is so easily adoptable. Like traditional consulting, it doesn't require the executive to be technical. This is not the computer, adopted from below by whoever could type and terminal. You just talk to your consultant, and it handles the technical know-how. This adoption wave can start from the corner office. And the consultancy, in turn, can accelerate AI adoption across the rest of the org.

### The evolving employment contract

One reason looms large for why firms distrust remote work and contracting: enforceability. There has never been a cheap way for firms to observe the doing of work in its deserving richness. So they measure pantomimes of it: clicks counted, mouse wiggles, green status dots, signals so divorced from whether good work is happening.

AI offers a monitor that understands. It can look at what a person produces and recognize real progress towards bigger objectives. As we explore in the next section, any new surveillance mustn't be implemented lightly. But, under the right circumstances, it can increase trust and encourage firms to give their employees more freedom.

If the monitor comprehends the many disparate paths that can lead to good work product, employees can work more the way they want. Progress toward good results will matter more, rather than one prescribed method easy for bosses to track. An intelligent monitor affords flexibility and punishes the performative productivity we all loathe.
[Figure caption: Six maps show different routes across the same landscape.]

The value proposition here resembles that of the insurers who reduce your rates if you install a tracker in your car. If you were trying to game the system, this hurts you; if you were simply doing your job, the monitor can vindicate you, and management trusts you more.

That trust is generative. We might see new willingness from firms that were previously reluctant to hybrid work, to unorthodox minds, to contractors, or to hiring brilliant people in countries far away.

There are many problems that ground-floor employees have with the org chart today. It is immensely dispiriting to have no way to surface a problem up the ladder that we see firsthand. It's a raw deal to be stuck under an ineffectual manager, or to do great work that goes unrecognized.

We see the previous wave of business hyperoptimization as being built on limited data, rough guidelines, and dumb algorithms. If all you can process is gear ratios, then everyone looks like a cog. Firms have long had a measurement problem of their workforce. An intelligent monitor could recognize valuable contributions and unique approaches that today's crude metrics miss.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
IV. Four big questions
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Putting our three stylized scenarios together, we now turn to a few questions that will define the future of the firm.

### 1. Will firms have more or less red tape?

Building is generally quite expensive. An app, a film, a deck, a feature, an analysis: these are all costly, slow, and agonizing to redo. When you effectively get one shot, you invest in aim. There are planning cycles and roadmap ceremonies and sign-off committees all to ensure that when the firm finally says go, it goes at something worthwhile. Many checks in our hierarchies exist because execution was precious.

Across many domains, AI is collapsing the cost of building—at least to the level of prototyping, if not to final completion. We believe the firm's approach should shift with it: aim less, fire more. A firm can build a prototype, observe, learn fast, reorient itself, and build it again. Easy prototyping should drift firms away from the institutions which underpin planning-intensive production.
[Figure caption: Sheets of a blueprint fold into a sequence of paper chairs.]

The simplest version of this is a team that iterates more quickly. In a more radical interpretation, the team itself is called into question. If collaborative planning matters less and uncorrelated diverse ideas matter more, is it better to have a single team that iterates quickly, or many individuals each putting forward their own working prototypes? If a real prototype transmits a vision better than any scoping document ever could, will our norms for communication change: sharing more, earlier, and sharing artifacts rather than memos?

Cheap building may tilt the firm toward individuals. This would mean fewer layers of middle management and less asking for permission, because asking used to be cheaper than doing and now it is not.

All that said, as we discuss next, the same technology which empowers individuals to prototype with less risk can also enforce larger spools of red tape.

### 2. Will workers have more or less agency?

A major reason firms enforce top-down management is the belief that executives make better business-relevant decisions than junior workers. Firms pay more for highly competent veterans at the top, and managers are often trained in business strategy while individual contributors are specialized in their expertise. If AI offers excellent advice to all workers, the gap in decision-making quality shrinks. There would be less reason for managers to meddle. This would increase worker autonomy, and potentially reduce layers of management.

Similarly, the “AI consultancy” ensures that nothing too consequential happens without an executive being alerted. If a worker is about to publish a blog post with serious repercussions for the company, the consultancy will proactively flag it to the exec. This begets trust: management need not worry big mishaps will happen without their awareness, so they needn't keep such a tight leash on how a worker practices their job.

But the AI consultancy is double-edged. Just as it acts as an intelligent monitor to give management more trust in workers, it can also enforce the CEO's preferred way of doing things more strongly across the org. Firms value coherence: not just a good decision, but a decision compatible with the company's culture, brand, and economies of scale. A restaurant chain may prefer a common menu across its franchises, so that ingredients can be bulk-ordered and customers can expect a consistent product.

Relatedly, the incentives for firm bosses don't always align with workers. Workers optimize for their domain's success while execs care about the firm overall. Equity owners have different preferences than wage workers, because they reap the rewards of harder work. The consultancy could enforce stricter adherence to specific regimens prescribed from the top.

Our view is, on net, workers will gain agency. Our optimism rests on firms using AI to flag consequential exceptions while leaving more decisions with workers.

We believe that AI will act as a scaffold—even if executives wish to maintain a coherent brand, this will merely be a constraint to operate within. Workers are made better aware of the business constraints at play and are better insured for creativity outside those restrictions. With their bases covered, execs can let employees try potentially lucrative new approaches. Supported by their AI helpers underneath, workers will also be responsible for a larger volume of output. We think directing more complex work will bring more opportunities for creativity.

### 3. Will firms shrink or widen?

The same logic on worker autonomy also applies to firm size. Ronald Coase asked why we organize production inside firms rather than through a web of contracts. Even an airplane could be constructed purely through peer-to-peer contracting, but it would be very inefficient. Firms muster economies of scale, align incentives, organize large projects, and absolve the need for constant bartering.

By easing communication, AI could justify larger or smaller firms. The classical firm is infrastructure for passing information up and instructions down, full of frictions and telephone-game. A larger firm (with more human employees) is less unwieldy if AI acts as a coordination layer. With the “AI consultancy,” an executive can know her firm the way a founder knows a twenty-person startup. She can watch an initiative's implementation rather than await its quarterly retelling, and be pinged if anything happens. Her vision can infuse more broadly. Everyone has a clearer sense of what everyone else is doing, and of the organization's overall priorities.

It could also go the other way. If communication is frictionless and compliance more monitorable, a firm needn't internalize so much of its supply chain. The firm can better trust outside suppliers and contractors.

A major motivation for vertical integration is to align incentives. There's the idea of incomplete contracts—an aerospace company could contract with a jet engine company, but for everything not explicitly required in the contract, the jet engine company is incentivized to cheap out and look after its own bottom line. To avoid this, the aerospace company buys the jet engine company. Just like with workers and bosses: if contracts are more monitorable and arbitrated intelligently rather than by checking off boxes, firms may be more comfortable relying on other firms. There would be less cause to do everything in-house.

We expect to see many smaller firms, but also sprawling AI “holding companies” where a huge number of different projects are conducted within one enterprise. The conglomerate is economical where there is some common asset leveraged by all the different projects. This common asset could be compute, though compute can easily be provisioned in small ad hoc quantities. A common factory or robot fleet is a better motivation.
[Figure caption: One crane lifts a roof among several projects along a quay.]

The large “holding company” can also be justified by aligned incentives. If a large organization of AIs and capital is entirely devoted to a common mission—say, curing cancer—there is less need for separate smaller initiatives. An AI firm could avoid power struggles, share all information, and, where needed, instantiate creative diversity among its agents so that different paths are explored.

### 4. Will pay disparity rise or fall?

Today's system is supposed to reward standout workers. They rise through the ranks and are better compensated. But in a world of superb model advisors, how will workers distinguish themselves?

If AI gives the same great idea to everyone, we have fewer opportunities to differentiate. Pay scales should compress.

Working against this compression are judgment, taste, and scope. If we are each managing AI firms underneath us, the remaining human value is in decision-making. If everyone is responsible for a bigger operation beneath them, the difference between good and bad judgment is amplified.

We have long applied this logic to the top of the org chart. We justify CEO pay with the idea that marginally better decisions up top can have huge effects on the bottom line. Pay inequality could increase if some workers better manage the AI firms beneath them to deploy scarce resources.

The more at stake, the more your small edge over someone else shows up in your paycheck. On the other hand, AI will give good advice to everyone. Less differentiation squishes the pay distribution.

Here and elsewhere, the prevailing force will perhaps hinge on whether we are mostly providing judgment or following it.
